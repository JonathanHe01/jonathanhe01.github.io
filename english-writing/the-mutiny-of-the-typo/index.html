The Mutiny of the Typo: Hamartia as Moral Surplus in Gilgamesh and the Gita

Lead-in

In the metaphysical architecture of the ancient world, the universe is characterized by bureaucracy rather than democracy. Be it the inscrutable Council of the Anunnaki in Mesopotamia or the supreme Lord Krishna in the Gita as the ‘author’, they always inflict a function-oriented script on human beings. A king is programmed to reign over the kingdom and resign to his immortality, whereas a warrior is programmed to slaughter and uphold his ‘dharma’. The cosmic in the literature is a closed loop of sheer indifference, where the tribulations of individuals are nothing but trivial frictions within the machinery. Remarkably, Soren Kierkegaard invites us to reflect on a ‘clerical error’ in a creation which is framed as a ‘slip of pen’ reluctant to be rectified by the ‘author’, howling ‘No, I will not be erased, I will stand as a witness against thee, that thou art a very poor writer!’. The metaphor, albeit being a bit grotesque, serves as our license to anatomize the protagonists of The Epic of Gilgamesh and the Gita.

Trivially, you may interpret the flaws or ‘hamartia’ of Gilgamesh and Arjuna as hauteur and cowardice (attachment to kinship). Still, upon closer examination, their hamartia is essentially an ontological surplus rather than handicapped characters. To wit, they run counter to the ‘author’ through possessing a surplus of morality, which takes form by radical particularity in Gilgamesh’s case and compassion in Arjuna’s case. They rejected succumbing to the divine script, which would ineluctably transform them into indifferent machineries.

Gilgamesh: The Glitch of Radical Particularity

Let’s put a caveat here: The prologue did reveal Gilgamesh as the author of his own epic, whereas our thesis identifies it as otherness instead of Gilgamesh in the first place. I didn’t misrepresent the masterpiece, as the initially specious definition serves as the cornerstone for my logic. However, we will sleep on it and make do with the current definition. As one may see, Gilgamesh was instilled into humanity as a hybrid entity: "Two-thirds of him was god, but a third of him was human" (Gilgamesh, I 48), which we can chalk up to his glitch. Keep in mind that Gilgamesh is expected to build Uruk’s walls and exit the stage when the time is opportune, since it’s the very yardstick we measure his flaws against. The "slip of the pen" occurs at the moment of Enkidu’s demise. Gilgamesh, being obsessed with a counterpart (Enkidu) who shares the same formidable power as him, would naturally grieve over Enkidu’s demise. Yet, he goes far beyond mere mourning. He refuses to surrender the corpse to the earth and tells the tavern-keeper Siduri, "I refused to let them bury him / in case my friend stood up at my cries" (Gilgamesh, X 60-61). The ‘editorial politics’ of the cosmos rule that humans are akin to leaves on the branch, interchangeable, ephemeral, and death is reduced to a process called ‘purge’. Nonetheless, Gilgamesh over-indexes on the life of Enkidu, which apparently runs counter to the pre-ordained transitoriness of creatures. Here, Gilgamesh is exactly the ‘error’ in Soren Kierkegaard’s epigram.

Ensue, the ‘editors’ of the text attempt to set Gilgamesh straight. Beholding the demise of Enkidu, Gilgamesh himself is fraught with fear of death and thereby embarks on the journey to seek immortality, which we all know is against the fiat of transitoriness. Let’s delve into how his attempt is dismissed. Utnapishtim, the immortal as the proxy of the ‘author’ meets Gilgamesh with phlegmatic logic rather than empathy, asks, "Do we build a house to last for ever? / Do we seal a contract to last for ever?" (Gilgamesh, X 306-307) and seals the argument that human life is as fragile as a dragonfly drifting down the river" (Gilgamesh, X 319). Here, the ‘author’ is speaking through Utnapishtim, demanding that Gilgamesh, the ‘typo’, cease struggling and accept his destiny.

Apparently, the philosophical gravity of Gilgamesh’s revolt lies in his violent rejection of interchangeability. By clinging to a specific, non-transferable loss, Gilgamesh, the "clerical error", disavows the administrative logic: death is merely a personnel rotation to maintain the equilibrium of the cosmos. He posits that a human companion is not a "house" to be demolished upon lease expiry, but a singularity immune to interchangeability, thereby exposing the tyranny of the gods’ prioritizing function over sentiments.

To disabuse your scepticism of the identity of the ‘author’, I would resort to the transformation of the ‘author’, assuming you are aware of the failure of Gilgamesh’s revolt. During Gilgamesh’s ordeal, he has yet to record his epic, and the ‘author’ figuratively refers to the metaphysical powers in the story, be it Utnapishtim or the fiat of fate. The metaphysical ‘author’ codes the scripts of mortality to trap Gilgamesh. Remarkably, the Kierkegaardian metaphor evolves upon Gilgamesh’s resignation to the fate of death and reinstatement as a functional king of Uruk; the 'clerical error' does not merely scream at the ‘author’ but seizes the ‘pen’ and usurps the position of ‘author’. Here, Gilgamesh alights upon that he can write the historical script of his ordeals himself, while he cannot edit the metaphysical script of the gods. By carving his grief onto lapis lazuli, which is at most he can do within his purview, he succumbs to the script but forces the universe to bear his complaint in mind for good. If you are cunning enough to detect the transition from ‘Bad Writers’ to ‘Good Writers’, then the contradictory identity of the ‘author’ is unravelled naturally with aptness! Moreover, Gilgamesh perfectly stands as a witness in the most literal sense: preserving the memory of his own mutiny against the silence of nature.

Arjuna: The glitch of compassion

If Gilgamesh revolts against the interchangeability and transitoriness of beings, Arjuna revolts against the onus of slaughter. In the Bhagavad Gita, the ‘author’, rather than being an intangible scribe, is materialized as the Lord of Krishna, namely the charioteer holding the reins. As the incarnation of time and space, Krishna inflicts a script on Gilgamesh rooted in the rigid and unequivocal ‘dharma’ of a warrior: identify the enemies, engage in the war valiantly, and slaughter every last one of them. The "clerical error" emerges in the opening scene, the ‘Arjuna-Vishada Yoga’ (The Yoga of Dejection). At the precise moment when conch shells have sounded, and the weapons are primed, Arjuna ‘flinches’. "My limbs sink, / my mouth dries up... / and Gandiva slips from my hand" (Bhagavad Gita, p. 7, 1:29-30). Chafed by Arjuna’s inaction, Krishna reprimands him almost immediately: "Whence has this stain / of spirit come to you... / an-Aryan, heaven-barring?" (Bhagavad Gita, p. 18, 2:2). To the author, this indecision amounts to an ignoble flaw, a malfeasance in the flow of divine sentence.

Nonetheless, Kierkegaard’s epigram vindicates Arjuna’s despondency as a surplus of vision. While a ‘perfect’ warrior is overwhelmed by targets and obligations, Arjuna, the ‘glitch’, sees way beyond that, a kinship not to be taken lightly. He identifies his enemies as "fathers, grandfathers, teachers, maternal uncles, brothers" (Bhagavad Gita, p. 6, 1:26). His flaw lies in expanding the circle of moral concern beyond the utilitarian boundaries of the battlefield and interrogating indignantly with an ethical question: "how can we be happy, / Madhava, if we kill our own kin?" (Bhagavad Gita, p. 8, 1:37).

It would be thoughtless and unfair to ascribe Arjuna’s hesitation to the cowardice of a man who fears death, since he would otherwise have no reason to offer to sacrifice himself to ward off the title ‘executioner’: "It would be more happiness for me / if the sons of Dhritarashtra / should kill me... / unarmed and unresisting" (Bhagavad Gita, p. 9, 1:46). By casting down his bow, the "clerical error" attempts to rewrite the genre of the text from a heroic epic to a pacifist tragedy. He stands as a witness, unveiling the gory nature of the order itself, which suggests fidelity even at the cost of murdering the warrior’s own family.

While Arjuna’s revolt ends up as a failure, it’s hard to overstate the philosophical gravity of his revolt, his steadfast rejection of ‘instrumentality’. The ‘author’, Krishna, rules that souls are eternal, bodies are merely "clothes" to be discarded, and the warrior is simply a tool of Time(Bhagavad Gita, p. 21, 2:22), which exactly captures the connotation of ‘instrumentality’. Arjuna rejects the glorified ‘dharma’ that renders violence palatable by prioritizing the "horizontal" bonds of human kinship over the "vertical" command of ‘dharma’. His ‘hamartia’ exposes the horrendous nonchalance of a universe where human beings are mere variables in an equation of cosmic balance. Throughout the eighteen chapters, the ‘error’ is devoted to proving that the human capacity for compassion is the only friction capable of slowing the terrifying fiat of fate.

The Conversation between Gilgamesh and Arjuna: Convergence and Divergence

Gilgamesh’s and Arjuna’s journeys converge on a communal enemy: the indifference of the universe, when placed in conversation. As ‘clerical errors’, Gilgamesh interrogates the silence of nature by screaming against the preposterous deletion of a conscious being without consent, and Arjuna interrogates the violence of duty by trembling upon witnessing the gory fratricide. It’s worth noting that the author’s defence is appallingly identical in its coldness. Utnapishtim, embodying the divine order of the gods, dismisses humans’ pursuit of immortality by comparing life to a "dragonfly" that drifts briefly down the river before vanishing (Gilgamesh, X 319). Lord Krishna, embodying the invincible flow of time, belittles human death by comparing the body to "worn-out clothes" that the soul discards (Bhagavad Gita, p. 21, 2:22). Both metaphors converge to the same worldview: the individual is nothing but a temporary vessel designed to facilitate the flow of nature.

However, I am particularly devoted to pinpointing how they diverge: their revolts end up in a vastly different manner. Arjuna is silenced by the overflow of the divine vision and dissolves back into Krishna’s narrative as a functional and indifferent warrior. Gilgamesh, on the contrary, ends up in a more unorthodox and subversive way. He does succumb to his mortal fate, but he then seizes the stylus to carve his hardships onto lapis lazuli; he metamorphoses from the passive 'error' in the gods' text into the active 'author' of the human testament. To wit, Arjuna finds peace by becoming the arrow, whereas Gilgamesh finds dignity by becoming the tablet.

Refutations and Defence

Since Gilgamesh, as the author, determines the flow of the epic as per his own inclination, we anticipate there would be cynics dismissing him as a narcissist, claiming, "Of course, he is the hero of his own story; he wrote it! That is the ultimate egoism." Ironically, it is the very authorship that exonerates him from the charge of narcissism. A vainglorious author would have edited out the hero’s failures and just claimed he won immortality. By recording his own weeping, his filth, and his failure, Gilgamesh vindicates his motivation as the preservation of a difficult truth rather than self-aggrandizement. To wit, Gilgamesh wrote a confession instead of a victory.

As for Arjuna, his revolt might be dismissed by utilitarians as nothing since he didn’t go as radical as Gilgamesh and simply succumbed. We, the readers, are left haunted not by his conformity but by his recalcitrance. We do not gravitate towards the Gita merely for Krishna’s sermon, but for Arjuna’s desperate, humanistic questions that precipitated it. The "clerical error" may have been erased from the plot, but it remains indelible in the meaning. Arjuna contends that to be wholly human is to be "wrong" in the eyes of the universe, and while the Gods possess the omnipotence to dictate the universe, only the flawed, broken human possesses the dignity to question it.
