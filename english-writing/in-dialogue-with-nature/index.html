In dialogue with nature

In this essay, we will mainly focus on how Plato and Aristotle could contribute to the discovery of the laws of motion, which is an enduring and ascending process rather than an immediate process. Although Plato and Aristotle diverged on the definition of true reality, they both facilitated the subsequent studies on motion to a certain degree.

In terms of Plato, his famous Allegory of the Cave describes human perception as limited to shadows of reality, suggesting that observation alone is insufficient for discovering truth. Beyond the cave is his world of forms, where true reality lies, reflects Plato’s methodology to acquire true knowledge through abstract reasoning and mathematical idealization. 1This facilitates the discovery of true laws governing motion. Aristotle proposed an imperfect explanation of motion purely based on his daily observations, but there were substantial observations that served as counterexamples. For instance, his ‘natural motion’ and ‘forced motion’ model could not explain that an object would continue moving without coercion because it would naturally stop once it reaches its natural destination based on Aristotle’s model. 2On the contrary, Galileo Galilei and Newton quantitatively approached motion with mathematical methods, successfully refining the inherent flaws of Aristotle’s philosophy. This could be interpreted as a vivid application of Plato’s methodology. Meanwhile, Plato valued rational deduction over physical experiments, whose approach aligns with Galileo’s and Newton’s thought experiments. Galileo conducted a thought experiment to disprove Aristotle’s assertion: If an object with one mass is tied to another with two masses, then the velocity of the composite should fall in between the velocity of each object, which contradicts the assumption that falling speed is proportional to the mass. For Newton, he indeed found the law of gravity partly by the Cannonball thought experiments: Suppose a cannonball is located high in position and then fired with different speeds, then the cannonball would eventually fall to the ground due to gravity. If the cannonball is fired at sufficiently large speed, then it could revolve around the earth without falling to the ground.3 Both Galileo and Newton followed a Platonic methodology of abstract reasoning before empirical testing.

Now, we proceed to Aristotle. By no means should we diminish his contribution to the law of motion. Although Aristotle failed to generate a quantitative and true underlying law of motion, he did postulate substantial theories that served as the inspiration for subsequent scholars. Overall, Aristotle was the first to systematically classify different motions as ‘natural motion’ and ‘forced motion’. He acutely noticed that the coercion exerted by outer forces could make a difference compared with inner-driven motion. For instance, Newton’s first law, also the Principle of Inertia, suggests that an object would persist in its moving state unless acted on by a net external force, and Newton’s second law suggests that the acceleration of a given object is proportional to the force exerted on it. They explicitly reflect this inherence from Aristotle’s theories by differentiating uniform motion without forces and accelerating motion caused by forces. Also, Aristotle developed the concept of resistance and clarified the roles of motive force and resistance. He even tried to explain motion quantitatively, though not as professionally as Galileo and Newton. For instance, he held that the time to cover a given distance during descending for an object is inversely proportional to its weight and proportional to the density of medium, which may not be correct, but provided a prototype. In that case, Book two of Newton’s ‘Principia’ discussing the motion of bodies through resisting medium, namely fluid mechanics, reflected Aristotle’s influence on him. Moreover, Aristotle identified the unmoved mover for the motion in the celestial region as the “Prime Mover”, which also found its echo in Newton’s exploration of planetary motion and law of gravity. It could be concluded that Aristotle provided a prototype for modern physics, without which subsequent scholars couldn’t locate a starting point. Inspiration is ignited during the process of scrutinizing Aristotle’s philosophy and then refining them.

Aristotle, coupled with Plato, provided a plausible methodology of exploration of the human universe. Overemphasis on abstract reasoning would result in a lack of contact with the sense world and corporeal objects since the theory stemming from pure logic should stand up to the scrutiny of real experiments and observations after all. Meanwhile, overemphasis on sense experience and observations would result in an inconclusive and unsystematic theory. One should apply logical reasoning like deduction to extract a universal feature from cumulative observations rather than simply assume by induction.
