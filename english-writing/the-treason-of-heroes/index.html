The Treason of Heroes

"Monkey mothers sang, baby monkeys cried: this was the concert of songs and drums that always thundered for Humbaba." (Gilgamesh, V 24–26)

Gilgamesh and Enkidu are both portrayed as physically formidable and mentally ambitious from the outset, but being satisfied with that definition is superficial. Indeed, a comprehensive silhouette of them wouldn’t take shape until their conquest of Humbaba. At first sight, their mission seems a righteous act of taming the savage and a way to pursue immortality through securing their names in history for valiant combat against the ‘monster’ Humbaba. Nonetheless, if you dive into tablet V, that glorified image would be shattered immediately.

The line of interest, as a license for us to pinpoint the flaws of Gilgamesh and Enkidu, is characterized by its vocabulary of a civilized society. The sounds in Cedar Forest are not chaotic noises but rather a symphony. Animals are personified as ‘sang’ and ‘cried’, which is the language of Uruk, implying that Cedar Forest is a parallel civilization by measure of Uruk (Gilgamesh, V 24–26). Moreover, ‘always thundered for Humbaba’ may find its echo also in Uruk. To wit, Shamhat introduced Enkidu to civilization by describing Uruk as the place "where the drums thunder on" (Gilgamesh, I 229) and Gilgamesh commanded his people to "let the drums thunder for Ninsun!" (Gilgamesh, II 271). Therefore, the forest itself is a harmonious and self-contained society with its own rituals. Humbaba, perceived as monstrous by Gilgamesh, is nothing more than a legitimate, divine guardian who keeps Cedar from intruders and hazards. The drums are a hallmark of his position as a respectable patron. Now it suffices to reveal their mission as a sheer colonial suppression to silence the ‘concert’ in Cedar, motivated by their hauteur as deities who regard others as nothing but monsters to be slain or resources to be plundered. Their deeds are further malicious upon recognizing the forest as essentially a nursery, as implied by ‘mothers’ and ‘baby’.

However, it would be thoughtless to lump Gilgamesh and Enkidu together as a unit, since what Cedar is to Enkidu is nothing the same as what Cedar is to Gilgamesh. The forest is alien to Gilgamesh, whereas Enkidu himself originates from the forest as a ‘savage’. He was "born in the wild" (Gilgamesh, II 177), "grazed on grass" with the gazelles (Gilgamesh, I 110), and was a "child of the mountains" (Gilgamesh, I 174). Ironically, Enkidu could have partaken in the ‘concert of songs and drums’ he then destroyed, which instilled more connotations into his intrusion as being a self-betrayal compared with Gilgamesh. Trivially, you may interpret Enkidu’s death as a comeuppance for his crime. But by virtue of this novel recognition, we may interpret his death more profoundly as a symmetrical balance. He abandoned the wild, so he must die and return to it as "clay" (Gilgamesh, X 68).

As for Gilgamesh, it would be a pitfall to ascribe his hauteur to a weakness of humanity, as one may find Gilgamesh to be two-thirds god and one-third human (Gilgamesh, I 48). However, it is his divinity that contributes to his restless mindset and hauteur to slay Humbaba and endows him with a surplus of power to disturb the order of nature. Humanity, rather than a weakness, is more like a solution. It is his humanity that allows him to grieve. Gilgamesh's divine arrogance is shattered upon Enkidu’s death, and his previously hidden humanity takes form by "grief has stepped into my heart" (Gilgamesh, IX 4). The mixed fraction of Gilgamesh also aligns well with the theme of an epic where humanity is instilled into the gods.
